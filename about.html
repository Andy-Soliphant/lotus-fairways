Koh Yao Yai, Thailand · Early 2000s

There is an island in Phang Nga Bay — tucked between Phuket and Krabi, largely invisible to the traveller in a hurry — called Koh Yao Yai. In the early 2000s, it was a different world entirely. No electricity after dark. A local man who looked after us completely — cooked our meals, gave beach massages as monkeys played in the trees above, built fires on the sand each evening as the last light left the sky.

One evening, I ordered wine and cheese. The owner came to find me — not embarrassed, just gently puzzled — to explain that the island had neither. Both were on the mainland. He could send word by boat. It would take two days.

"I'm not in a rush. I'll wait."

Two days later, it arrived. And when it did, the whole island seemed to know. Staff and locals came running down to the beach — not to serve the order, but to share it. Wine and cheese, passed around in the firelight, on a Thai island that had gone to extraordinary lengths simply because a guest had asked.

That is Southeast Asia at its truest. Not a service standard. Not a brand promise. A genuine, unhurried, warm-hearted response that went so far beyond the expected that thirty years later it remains one of the most vivid memories of a lifetime of travel. It is also, in a very real sense, why Lotus & Fairways exists.

There are more stories — islands in Thailand where money wasn't used at all, a Vietnamese village where we were the first Englishmen anyone had met, golf courses played at dawn with no one else on earth in sight. We're writing them up in our Journal as we go.
