A Safari That Never Happened

The Cardamom Mountains · 6 min read

Cambodia · The Cardamoms

In November 1967, Jacqueline Kennedy went to Cambodia as the guest of King Norodom Sihanouk. She had wanted to see Angkor Wat since she was a girl and said as much when she got there. It was a strange piece of Cold War diplomacy — a former First Lady in a country the war next door was about to reach — and it is well documented. She saw the temples. She went home.

Fifty years later, an architect in Bangkok asked a different question. What if Sihanouk had not taken her to Angkor at all? What if he had taken her south instead, into the Cardamom Mountains, on safari? Where would they have camped? How would she have arrived? What would the tents have looked like?

"This was my driving inspiration and the DNA behind Shinta Mani Wild."

That is Bill Bensley, describing the thought that produced the camp. It is worth pausing on how odd a starting point that is. Not a brief, not a brand standard, not a market position. A guest list for a journey nobody took.

Fifteen tents, and no two the same

The camp has fifteen tents strung along a river in the southern Cardamoms, and every one is different. The first built pays its respects to Cambodian royalty and set the pattern for everything after it. Another is given over to Jackie's real 1967 visit, and is full of the ephemera of it. Others belong to the animals of the forest, to the explorers and naturalists who catalogued this part of the world, and to the rangers who keep it now.

Inside, the furniture is what a royal party might have travelled with: campaign chests, a botanical press, an old sewing machine, antiques he and his partner have spent years buying at European auctions. It could very easily have been ridiculous. It is not, because the fiction is followed absolutely and never explained.

Guests arrive by zip-line, four hundred metres or so over the water, to a bar at the landing zone. You can take the jeep track instead. Almost nobody does.

The part that is not a story

The land itself was going to be logged, and then mined for titanium. Bensley bought it — with Sokoun Chanpreda, his business partner of thirty years and the founder of the Shinta Mani Foundation — to stop that happening. The figures are usually given as around 865 acres, several kilometres of river, three waterfalls and something in the order of 875,000 trees.

Then he built a camp on it to pay for keeping it. That is the whole arrangement: fifteen tents across the entire valley, roughly one to every sixty-odd acres, placed over years so that no tree had to come down. He calls it minimal intervention. Here it is closer to a condition of the project.

The camp funds a Wildlife Alliance ranger station inside its own grounds. Guests can go out with the rangers, which means walking into the forest to pull snares, seize equipment and sometimes find people who should not be there. It is the opposite of a resort activity, and it is the single most memorable thing most people do here. A number of the staff — the foragers particularly, who know the forest better than anyone — were poachers and loggers before the camp existed.

"Conservation can be more profitable, both economically and ecologically, than extraction. The camp exists to prove it."

What it is actually like

Better than it sounds, and less severe. Everything is included — meals, drinks, the activities, the transfers, the spa, which is unusual anywhere and welcome in a place three hours from the nearest city. There is a pool above the main cascade. There are bars that appear in the forest when somebody decides there should be one. The adventure is entirely optional: some guests spend three days boulder-hopping upriver, and others do not leave the deck above the water.

It sits between three national parks, a few hours south of Phnom Penh, and it opened at the end of 2018. Three nights is the right length. Two is a shame.

Where it sits in the bigger picture

Shinta Mani Wild is one of eight Bensley hotels on a journey we have put together across Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam — nineteen nights, twelve guests at the very most, one departure in December 2027. Three of those nights are here, which is deliberate. After the temples and before Vietnam, the forest is where the journey slows down.

Written from our own stays in Cambodia. The rangers are real; the safari never happened.
