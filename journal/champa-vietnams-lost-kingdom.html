Champa: Vietnam’s Lost Kingdom

Vietnam · Hoi An · Quy Nhon · Ninh Thuan · 8 min read

Vietnam · History

Drive the coast road south from Da Nang and every so often a tower appears in a field or on a hill: red brick, carved, older than anything around it, and usually with nobody standing in front of it. Most people see them from a car window on the way to a beach. They are the last standing buildings of a kingdom that ran this coast for more than a thousand years, sacked the greatest city in Southeast Asia, traded as far as Baghdad, and then almost vanished.

It was called Champa. Very few British travellers have heard of it, which is a large part of why it is worth knowing.

Who were the Cham?

The Cham built a kingdom along what is now central and southern Vietnam from around the second century AD until the fifteenth. It was never one state so much as a chain of city-ports along the coast, bound by the same language, the same gods and the same sea. Their religion came from India — Shiva above all — and so did their sacred language, Sanskrit. They also wrote their own: the oldest surviving text in any Southeast Asian language is Cham.

Above all they were seafarers. From the seventh century to the tenth, Cham ships carried the trade in spices and silk between China, India, the islands of Indonesia and Abbasid Baghdad, and when trade was slow they raided. They were the connective tissue of ancient Asian commerce, and the coast of central Vietnam was theirs because the sea was.

“A kingdom that sacked Angkor, traded with Baghdad, and is now a scatter of towers most people pass without stopping.”

The year they took Angkor

In 1177 a Cham fleet sailed up the Mekong, into the great lake of the Tonlé Sap, and fell on Yasodharapura, the Khmer capital at Angkor. They took the city and killed the Khmer king. Six years earlier, a Chinese instructor had taught the Cham to build siege crossbows, and they brought them with them.

The Khmer answer came under Jayavarman VII, who drove the Cham out and built the Bayon. On its walls the naval battles between Khmer and Cham are carved in stone, boats and all — which means that anyone who has stood at Angkor has already met the Cham, usually without being told who they were.

A trader from the Middle East, buried in 1039

The trade story is not a flourish; the evidence is in the ground. At Phan Rang, in the far south of the old kingdom, two gravestones cut in Kufic Arabic and dated 1039 mark the tomb of a Muslim merchant named Abu Kamil. It is hard proof of a Muslim trading community living on this coast nearly a thousand years ago — and it explains why the Cham communities of Ninh Thuan today are both Hindu, the Balamon, and Muslim, the Bani.

Further north, twenty kilometres off Hoi An, lie the Cham Islands, Cù Lao Chàm. The name comes from Pulau Champa, the island of Champa. Between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries it was a watering stop on the maritime route between the Middle East, India and China, and excavations at Bãi Làng have turned up Islamic ceramics from Middle and Near Eastern kilns, and Middle Eastern glass. The archipelago and the old town of Hoi An together now form a UNESCO biosphere reserve.

The fall of Vijaya, 1471

The Cham fought the Vietnamese to the north for centuries, winning and losing ground. The end came at Vijaya, the last great capital, near modern Quy Nhon. In 1471 the armies of Dai Viet took the city: sixty thousand Cham killed, thirty thousand taken as slaves, the capital destroyed. A remnant principality survived in the south until 1832, but the kingdom as a power ended in that valley.

What stands there now are the Bánh Ít towers on their hill above the plain. The hinge of the whole story happened within sight of them, and on most mornings you will have them to yourself.

The Cham today

The Cham did not disappear. The largest Cham population in the world still lives on the plains of Ninh Thuan, around Phan Rang, and some of the towers are working temples rather than monuments. Po Klong Garai was founded by Jaya Simhavarman III, the king remembered for trading two provinces to the Vietnamese in exchange for a princess. Po Nagar, above the river at Nha Trang, has been a place of worship for more than twelve hundred years — and was burned by raiders from Java in 774, a reminder of how far this sea world reached in both directions.

Each autumn, in the seventh month of the Cham calendar — usually late September or October — the Kate festival brings the Balamon communities to their towers to honour gods and ancestors. It is the one time of year the temples are full.

The modern history is harder. Cham communities also live along the Mekong in Cambodia, and under the Khmer Rouge an estimated hundred thousand Cham were killed; in 2018 the tribunal in Phnom Penh ruled it genocide. It is part of the story, and anyone travelling in the region should know it.

Where to see what survives

The Cham sites worth the journey

My Son
The sacred heart of the kingdom, in a bowl of hills an hour from Hoi An. A UNESCO World Heritage Site. Go at first light, before the coaches.
Museum of Cham Sculpture
Da Nang. The finest collection of Cham sculpture anywhere, and the best single hour you can spend understanding what you will see at the sites.
Cù Lao Chàm
The Cham Islands, off Hoi An. Boats sail reliably from about March to August; outside that the sea closes them, and the border authorities, not the boat operators, decide. Take a private boat rather than the crowded public run, and bring your passport.
Bánh Ít
Near Quy Nhon. Four towers on a hill above the site of Vijaya, the last capital.
Po Nagar
Nha Trang. Still a working temple after twelve centuries.
Po Klong Garai
Phan Rang. A working Cham temple, in the heartland of the Cham who live here now.

Where to stay along the way

The sites run down roughly four hundred kilometres of coast, which means three bases cover all of it.

Hoi An. Namia River Retreat sits on an islet in the Thu Bon, ten minutes by bicycle from the old town and well placed for My Son at dawn. Its kitchen forages herbs from the Cham Islands, which is the kind of detail that makes the thread feel continuous rather than staged.

Quy Nhon. Anantara Quy Nhon Villas, all villas on a private bay south of the city. Almost nobody from Britain comes here. You stand at Bánh Ít in the morning and are on an empty beach in the afternoon.

Vinh Hy Bay. Amanoi, in the Núi Chúa national park above the bay, with the Cham plain of Ninh Thuan below it. Amanoi runs its own Cham programme, including an evening with a Cham feast and a blessing given by a Cham master under the stars.

Seeing it properly

The towers can be seen in a day or two each, on your own, and they are worth it even that way. But the story only makes sense in order — the sanctuary, then the trade, then the fall, then the people who are still here — and with someone beside you who can read the stones.

That is the journey we have built. The Cham Tour runs eleven nights from Hoi An to Vinh Hy Bay, north to south, in the order the history happened, with a historian and our founder travelling with a small group throughout.
