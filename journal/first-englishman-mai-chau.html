The First Englishman in the Village

Vietnam · Mai Chau · 6 min read

Vietnam · Mai Chau

I was living in Hanoi, and I was looking for somewhere quiet. I am a village boy at heart, and big cities wear me out faster than they seem to wear out other people. It is the traffic noise that does it. After a while the need for greenery and silence stops being a preference and starts being something closer to a requirement.

I had done the places everyone does. Ha Long Bay. Hoi An. Da Nang. Plenty more would come later. But somewhere in among them I came across a name I did not know — Mai Chau — described as off the beaten track, though not so far off that nobody had been. That sounded about right. My mother was over visiting, so the two of us went.

Down a lane, off a smaller lane

The drive took around three hours. As we came into the valley the view did the thing that good views do, which is to stop the conversation in the car. Rice terraces, one below the next, running away into the distance until you lose track of where they stop.

I assumed we were nearly at the homestay I had booked. I was wrong. We kept going. There were several phone calls between the driver and someone at the other end, and then we turned into a small lane, and off that into a smaller one, and off that into a smaller one again — a bit like Russian dolls. At the end of the last of them was a traditional wooden house.

The welcome was immediate and warm, in the way that has never once surprised me in the Vietnamese countryside and has never once stopped being worth remarking on. We were shown to where we would sleep, which turned out not to be a room in the main house but a house of our own, just for the two of us. The bedding was basic. Everything else was extraordinary.

Then the family introduced themselves, all of them: mother and father, the children including the youngest boy, and the grandparents. We sat down with them and ate some of what they had prepared. I still think about the betel leaf rolls.

The afternoon

We walked. That was the whole plan and it was the right one. The area is farmland and it is famous for being farmland, and it is one of the greenest places I have been anywhere in Asia.

We passed children playing in the water who greeted us with enormous smiles and a volume of HELLO that I can still hear. We passed people working, and the bamboo waterwheels that lift water up into the paddies and have been doing it in that valley for a very long time.

My quest for somewhere green and quiet was, by this point, comprehensively achieved.

Dinner, and the rice wine

We came back to the smell of cooking and to the family telling us we would be eating with them. Dinner was an assortment of dishes, some of which suited me better than others. The thing I will not forget is the youngest boy, no more than three years old, working his way through two deep-fried chicken heads as though they were lollipops, one in each hand. I stuck to the other dishes.

Afterwards the children went off to bed and we sat in a small room with an ancient television in the corner. It was not switched on, but I can still picture it, and I remember thinking about how much of the family's evenings it must have accounted for over the years.

The grandfather invited me to join the men for the local rice wine. I am not one to say no. It became clear fairly quickly that this is drunk fast, and more or less continuously.

We talked for hours in broken English and my considerably more broken Vietnamese, and got on famously in the way that people do when neither side can rely on the words and both sides are trying. There were a lot of stories and a great deal of laughing. And then, at some point after midnight, the grandfather announced that I was the first Englishman ever to come to his village. Nobody had been before.

"I told him not to worry. I was the best, and all the others would disappoint."

That went down about as well as anything I have ever said in my life. The rice wine, needless to say, kept coming. I made my way back to our own house in the early hours with a broad smile on my face and the day already filed away as one of the good ones.

The morning, and the years since

Breakfast was hearty and waiting for us. The shower was extremely cold. And then it was time to leave the family, which took longer than it should have, as these things do.

I have been back many times since. I have watched the valley change — more visitors, more homestays, hotels of a kind that did not exist when I first drove in. Some of that is good and some of it is the price of a place being found. It happens everywhere eventually, and Mai Chau has handled it better than most.

But whatever it becomes, it will always be the place I found when I needed to escape, and the village where I was the first Englishman to arrive.

If you are thinking of going

Mai Chau is roughly three hours from Hanoi by road, which makes it an easy two nights at the start or end of a northern Vietnam journey and a far better one than most of what sits at that distance from the capital. Go for the valley and the walking rather than for a list of sights, because there is not really a list. Stay in a homestay rather than a hotel if the idea appeals — the difference is not the bedding, it is the evening.

And if somebody offers you the rice wine, understand what you are agreeing to before you say yes.
