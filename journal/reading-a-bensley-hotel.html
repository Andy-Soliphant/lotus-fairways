Reading a Bill Bensley Hotel

Thailand · Cambodia · Laos · Vietnam · 6 min read

Southeast Asia · Design

The first time I stayed in a Bill Bensley hotel I did not know it was one. That is worth admitting, because it is how most people meet his work. You check in somewhere that feels unusually complete, you have a very good few days, and you leave without ever asking why the door handles matched the ceiling, or why the staff uniforms looked like they belonged to the building rather than to a brand.

Then you stay in a second one, in another country, and something nags. The same appetite for colour. The same refusal to leave a corner undecided. The same sense that somebody enjoyed themselves enormously.

He starts with a question, not a style

Bensley has said that his projects begin with a "what if". What if this hotel had once been a French university of natural sciences? What if the King of Cambodia had taken Jackie Kennedy on safari in 1968? What if Dutch colonists had pitched camp in the jungle above Ubud?

Those three questions produced JW Marriott Phu Quoc, Shinta Mani Wild and Capella Ubud. Once the story exists, everything follows from it — the buildings, the interiors, the signage, the uniforms, the name above the bar. It is why his hotels feel whole rather than decorated, and it is also why they are so enjoyable to stay in. You are inside somebody's idea, and the idea has been thought all the way through.

"Once you know he works this way, you stop looking at the room and start looking for the story."

At JW Marriott Phu Quoc, the fiction is a nineteenth-century academy named for the naturalist Lamarck, complete with departments, a chapel and a main street running through the resort. At Shinta Mani Wild, the tents carry the imagined luggage of a royal hunting party. None of it is explained to you on arrival. It is simply there, and you find it.

The conservation is not decoration either

The part of his work that gets written about least is the part he seems proudest of. At Capella Ubud he talked a client out of a 120-room hotel that would have cleared a sacred valley, and built twenty-odd tents on stilts instead, threaded between the trees so the drainage was never altered. He calls it minimal intervention: read the land, build around what is already there.

Shinta Mani Wild goes further. The camp sits on a stretch of the Cardamom Mountains that was scheduled to be logged and mined. Bensley bought the concession himself and built a camp to pay for protecting it instead. Guests go out with the Wildlife Alliance rangers who patrol the forest. The hotel exists to fund the thing it sits in, which is a rarer arrangement than the industry's brochures would suggest.

What to look for, once you know

Look at the ceilings. He treats them as surfaces rather than lids, and in several properties they carry the best work in the building. Look at what has been reused: antiques, salvaged shutters, old tiles bought at auction and fitted where they should not quite fit. Look at the colour, which is never timid and never random — each palette belongs to its story.

And look at the landscape first, not the architecture. He trained as a landscape architect, and it shows in the order things were decided: the garden was usually there before the building, and the building was placed to suit it.

Where to find his work

His hotels are scattered across Asia, which is part of the pleasure. The Siam in Bangkok is black and white art deco on the river, hung with a family's own antiques. Four Seasons Chiang Mai was his studio's first resort garden, laid out in 1992 in a disused quarry and now grown into a village around a working rice farm. Four Seasons Tented Camp Golden Triangle sits above the Ruak. Rosewood Luang Prabang is built around a waterfall. In Cambodia there is Shinta Mani Angkor and its ten-villa Bensley Collection, and Shinta Mani Wild in the Cardamoms. In Vietnam, Capella Hanoi imagines an opera house beside the real one, and the InterContinental Danang runs down a headland in four terraces with a funicular through them.

Beyond those, Capella Ubud in Bali, JW Marriott Phu Quoc in the far south of Vietnam, and more recently Four Seasons Koh Samui. There are many others, and the list keeps growing.

"We have stayed in these places ourselves. It is the only way we know whether a hotel is as good as its photographs."

Eight of them, in the order he built them

We have put together one journey that stays in eight of his hotels across four countries, arranged so the route begins at that Chiang Mai garden from 1992 and ends in Da Nang, where his own paintings hang above the sea. Nineteen nights, twelve guests at the very most, and one departure in December 2027.

It is the only way we know of to see the range of the work rather than one example of it — and to watch an imagination develop across thirty years and four countries, one night at a time.

Written from our own stays across Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam.
