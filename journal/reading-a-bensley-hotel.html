Hotels Designed by Bill Bensley

Thailand · Laos · Cambodia · Vietnam · 7 min read

Southeast Asia · Design

The first time I stayed in a Bill Bensley hotel I did not know it was one. That is how most people meet his work. You check in somewhere that feels unusually complete, you have a very good few days, and you leave without ever asking why the door handles matched the ceiling.

Then you stay in a second one, in another country, and something nags. The same appetite for colour. The same refusal to leave a corner undecided.

What is Bill Bensley known for?

Two things. The first is volume: more than two hundred hotels and resorts across some thirty countries, from a studio in Bangkok where he has worked since the 1980s. He trained as a landscape architect before anything else, which matters more than it sounds.

The second is method. His projects begin with a question rather than a style. What if this hotel had once been a French university of natural sciences? What if the King of Cambodia had taken Jackie Kennedy on safari in 1968? What if Dutch colonists had pitched camp in the jungle above Ubud?

Those three questions produced JW Marriott Phu Quoc, Shinta Mani Wild and Capella Ubud. Once the story exists, everything follows from it — buildings, interiors, signage, uniforms, the name above the bar. It is why his hotels feel whole rather than decorated.

"Once you know he works this way, you stop looking at the room and start looking for the story."

Which hotels were designed by Bill Bensley?

Far too many to list in full, but these are the ones worth travelling for, country by country.

Thailand. The Siam in Bangkok, black and white art deco on the river. Four Seasons Chiang Mai, where his studio built its first resort garden in 1992 out of a disused quarry. Four Seasons Tented Camp Golden Triangle, above the Ruak. InterContinental Khao Yai, built around old railway carriages. Four Seasons Koh Samui.

Cambodia. Shinta Mani Angkor in Siem Reap and its ten-villa Bensley Collection. Shinta Mani Wild, in the Cardamom Mountains.

Laos. Rosewood Luang Prabang, built around a waterfall on a wooded estate above the town.

Vietnam. Four of them — see below.

Indonesia. Capella Ubud, twenty-odd tents on stilts in a valley above the Wos river.

Elsewhere. JW Marriott Jeju in South Korea, Shinta Mani Mustang in Nepal, and a long list beyond Asia. His own Bangkok house, Baan Botanica, is the one nobody gets to book.

Which hotels in Vietnam were designed by Bill Bensley?

Four, and Forbes puts the number the same way.

  • InterContinental Danang Sun Peninsula Resort — his first in the country, opened 2012, four levels down a headland named Heaven, Sky, Earth and Sea, with a funicular running through them. Home to La Maison 1888, the only Michelin-starred restaurant in Da Nang.
  • JW Marriott Phu Quoc Emerald Bay — 2016, built as a nineteenth-century French university with departments, a chapel and a main street. At 234 rooms it is much the largest of the four.
  • Hotel de la Coupole, Sapa — French Indochine dressed in hill-tribe textiles, in the mountains below Fansipan.
  • Capella Hanoi — 47 rooms, no two alike, imagined as the lodging where the opera company stayed. It sits a few steps from the real opera house.

The conservation is not a decorative detail

The part of his work that gets written about least is the part he seems proudest of. At Capella Ubud he talked a client out of a 120-room hotel that would have cleared a sacred valley, and built twenty-odd tents on stilts instead, threaded between the trees so the drainage was never altered. He calls it minimal intervention: read the land, build around what is already there.

Shinta Mani Wild goes further. The camp sits on a stretch of the Cardamom Mountains that was scheduled to be logged and mined. Bensley bought the concession himself and built a camp to pay for protecting it instead. Guests go out with the Wildlife Alliance rangers who patrol the forest.

What to look for, once you know

Look at the ceilings. He treats them as surfaces rather than lids, and in several properties they carry the best work in the building. Look at what has been reused: antiques, salvaged shutters, old tiles bought at auction and fitted where they should not quite fit. Look at the colour, which is never timid and never random — each palette belongs to its story.

And look at the landscape before the architecture. The garden was usually there first, and the building was placed to suit it.

"We have stayed in these places ourselves. It is the only way we know whether a hotel is as good as its photographs."

Eight of them, in the order he built them

We have put together one journey that stays in eight of his hotels across four countries, arranged so the route begins at that Chiang Mai garden from 1992 and ends in Da Nang, where his own paintings hang above the sea. Nineteen nights, twelve guests at the very most, one departure in December 2027.

It is the only way we know of to see the range of the work rather than one example of it — and to watch an imagination develop across thirty years and four countries, one night at a time.

Written from our own stays across Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam.
