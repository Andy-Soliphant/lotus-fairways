The Quarry That Became a Garden

Mae Rim, Chiang Mai · 6 min read

Thailand · The North

Stay at Four Seasons Chiang Mai and the first thing you notice is the rice. Not a lawn, not a water feature: a working paddy, terraced down the valley floor, with pavilions set around it as though a village had grown up along the edge over a century or two. Buffalo in the field. Somebody planting.

It is entirely convincing, and none of it was there. In 1992 this was a disused quarry.

The first garden

The resort was the first hotel garden designed by Bensley Design Studios, and it is where everything that came afterwards starts. Bill Bensley trained as a landscape architect before he was anything else, and here he was working the way landscape architects do: shaping the ground first and letting the buildings answer to it, rather than the other way round.

Thirty-odd years is a long time in a garden. What was planted in 1992 is now mature, which is why the place does not read as a design at all. It reads as somewhere that has always been there. That is the hardest thing to achieve in this business, and it cannot be rushed or bought.

"It reads as somewhere that has always been there. That is the hardest thing to achieve, and it cannot be rushed."

A valley that was already being replanted

What makes the choice of site more interesting is what was happening in these hills at the time. The northern uplands had been badly deforested, and from the late 1960s the Thai royal family had been working, patiently and at scale, to change that.

The Royal Project, founded in 1969, set out to give hill communities crops worth growing in place of opium, and to put the forest back. Queen Sirikit's work ran alongside it and took a different route: in 1976 she founded the SUPPORT Foundation to revive Thai handicrafts and give rural households — weavers above all — a supplementary income from skills that were quietly disappearing. She travelled constantly through these provinces for decades doing it. She died in 2025, having spent most of a lifetime on the north and on the crafts that belong to it.

And a few minutes from the resort, in the same Mae Rim valley, is the Queen Sirikit Botanic Garden. It opened in 1993 as the Mae Sa Botanic Garden and was renamed in her honour the following year: a thousand hectares on the flank of Doi Suthep-Pui, most of it left as natural forest, the rest given over to Thailand's first botanic garden built to international standards.

So the quarry turned into rice terraces sits inside a much larger and much older piece of work — a whole valley being deliberately put back. You can stay here without knowing any of that. It is better if you do.

What to look for

Walk the terraces early, when the field is being worked and the light is still low. Look at how the pavilions are placed: none of them dominates the valley, and each is angled so that what you see from the terrace is the farm rather than another building.

Look at the level changes. A quarry leaves awkward ground, and the whole composition is an answer to that problem rather than an idea imposed on a flat site.

Then go up the road to the botanic garden, ideally with somebody who can name things, and look at what the same mountains hold when nobody has arranged them at all. An hour there changes how the resort reads on the walk back.

Where it sits in the bigger picture

Four Seasons Chiang Mai is where our Bensley journey begins in earnest — eight of his hotels across Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam, arranged in the order he built them. Starting at the 1992 garden is the whole point: everything that follows, the tented camps and the imagined opera houses, grows out of a man who thought about the ground first.

Nineteen nights, twelve guests at the very most, one departure in December 2027. Three of those nights are here.
