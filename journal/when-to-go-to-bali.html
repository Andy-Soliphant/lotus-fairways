When to Go to Bali, and When Not To

Indonesia · Bali · Planning · 6 min read

Bali · When to Go

Bali has two seasons, not four, and the difference between them matters less than most people think. The weather is warm all year, around thirty degrees on the coast and a little cooler in the hills around Ubud. What changes is the rain, the crowds and the price, and the calendar of Balinese ceremonies, which follows rules of its own.

The short answer

Best months: April, May, June and September, sometimes October. Dry, green and quieter.

Busiest: July and August, and Christmas to New Year.

Wettest: December to February, though rarely all day.

2027 dates to plan around: Nyepi on Monday 8 March, when the island closes for twenty-four hours, followed by the national Idul Fitri holiday.

The dry season: April to October

From April the rains ease, the air dries out and the rice fields are at their greenest. April, May and June are, for us, the best months on the island: settled weather, warm seas and none of the summer crowds. September is similar, and October is often fine too, with the first showers arriving towards the end of the month.

July and August are dry and beautiful, and they are also when Europe and Australia are on holiday. The south of the island is at its busiest, the roads around Ubud and Uluwatu slow down, and the best rooms go early. If school holidays mean you have to travel then, book well ahead and spend more of your time away from the south: the east coast, the Sidemen valley, or another island altogether.

The wet season: November to March

The wet season sounds worse than it is. Rain tends to come as heavy downpours in the afternoon or overnight, with clear mornings, and the island is lush and quiet. December to February are the wettest months, and the air is humid. Christmas and New Year are an exception to the quiet: they are among the busiest and most expensive weeks of the year.

For the right traveller, the shoulder months either side, November and March, are good value: green, uncrowded and noticeably cheaper. Sea conditions can be rougher, which matters if boat days or Lombok’s Gili islands are part of the plan, and treks up the volcanoes are best left to the dry season.

Nyepi: the day Bali stops

Once a year Bali does something no other holiday island would: it switches itself off. Nyepi, the Balinese new year, is a day of silence. From six in the morning until six the next morning nobody goes out, no traffic moves, lights are kept low, and the airport closes. Visitors stay inside their hotels, which carry on quietly for their guests.

In 2027 Nyepi falls on Monday 8 March. The night before brings the ogoh-ogoh parades, when villages carry huge papier-mâché demons through the streets before burning them, and that evening is one of the most memorable on the Balinese calendar. Some travellers plan around Nyepi; others plan to be there for it. Either way, nobody can arrive or leave that day.

In 2027 there is a second reason to watch these dates. Idul Fitri, the end of Ramadan, falls on 10 and 11 March, and with the national holidays around it much of Indonesia is on the move from 6 to 15 March. Flights across the country, including to Java, Lombok and Sumba, are full and expensive that week.

Galungan, and the ceremonies you will see anyway

Bali’s ceremonial calendar runs on a 210-day cycle, so the great festivals move against our calendar. The most visible is Galungan, when tall bamboo poles called penjor, hung with woven palm leaves, line every village street. It lasts ten days and ends with Kuningan. In 2027 Galungan falls on 13 January and again on 11 August. It is a lovely time to be on the island; just expect some family-run places to close for the main days, and roads to be busy with processions.

Beyond the big festivals there is a ceremony somewhere on Bali almost every day: a temple anniversary, a cremation, a tooth-filing. A good guide will know what is happening near you, and how to watch respectfully.

Beyond Bali

Java and Lombok follow much the same pattern as Bali, at their best from April to October. Sumba is drier from April, and its quieter months from January to March bring lower prices at the best hotels. If you are adding an island, see Bali, Beyond Bali.

Worth knowing before you go

  • Tourist levy: foreign visitors to Bali pay a one-off levy of IDR 150,000 per person (about £7), best paid online through the provincial government’s Love Bali site before you travel. We remind every guest.
  • Flights: there are no direct flights from the UK; most routes connect through the Gulf, Singapore or Kuala Lumpur.
  • Temples: a sarong and sash are needed at temples, and usually provided.
