The Thu Bon·The Cham Islands·Vinh Hy Bay
A story told on water, so it is travelled on water.

For three centuries the Cham controlled the trade in spices and silk between China, India, the islands of Indonesia and Abbasid Baghdad. They were seafarers, and when it suited them, pirates. Their kingdom ran down the coast of what is now central Vietnam from the second century until the fifteenth — brick and sandstone towers, Shiva, Sanskrit, and a script they invented for their own language. The oldest surviving text in any Southeast Asian language is Cham.

Then it went. Vijaya, the capital, fell in 1471 — sixty thousand killed, thirty thousand taken as slaves, the city obliterated. The last remnant held on until 1832. What survives is a scatter of towers along the Vietnamese coast, the finest collection of Cham sculpture in the world in a museum in Da Nang, and communities still living, still Cham, in Ninh Thuan.

Almost all of it stands within four hundred kilometres of coast, which is why this journey does not sprawl. Three places, two moves, eleven nights — run north to south so that the history arrives in the order it happened: the sanctuary first, then the fall, then the people who are still here.

Most travellers pass the towers on the road to somewhere else. This journey is the somewhere else.

The Journey

1Hoi An

The Sanctuary — Four Nights on the River

You arrive at Namia River Retreat, on an islet in the Thu Bon, and the first day asks nothing of you. People land from different places at different hours; the group becomes a group over dinner, not in a minibus.

Then My Son at first light, before the coaches — brick towers in a bowl of hills, built and rebuilt across a thousand years, the spiritual centre of the whole kingdom. A separate morning goes out to the Cham Islands, Cù Lao Chàm, whose name is simply Pulau Champa. We take a boat of our own rather than the public run. Excavations at Bãi Làng have turned up Islamic ceramics from Middle Eastern kilns and Persian glass: this is where the world arrived. On the way out on the last morning, the Cham Museum in Da Nang, which holds the finest collection of Cham sculpture anywhere.

2By Rail

The Vietage — South Along the Coast

Six hours south by train from Da Nang, in a carriage that holds twelve. There are ten of you, our founder and the historian, so it is simply ours. The line runs between the mountains and the sea for most of it, which is the reason to take it rather than fly. The transfer is not something to be got through. It is part of the journey.

3Quy Nhon

The Fall — Three Nights at the Last Capital

Anantara Quy Nhon Villas, on a bay of its own. Twenty minutes away stood Vijaya, the capital, and in 1471 the Vietnamese took it: sixty thousand dead, thirty thousand carried off, the city erased. The Bánh Ít towers on their hill are what is left, and standing in them is the point of coming here — the hinge of the whole story happened in this valley, and almost nobody visits.

Evenings are the other half of it: dinner on the beach, which our founder has hosted here before, the restaurants at Avani+ next door, and the spa, which is built into the days rather than left to chance. On the drive south, Po Nagar at Nha Trang — a tower still in use as a place of worship after twelve hundred years.

4Vinh Hy Bay

The Survivors — Four Nights in Ninh Thuan

Amanoi, above Vinh Hy Bay in the Núi Chúa national park, and the largest surviving Cham population in the world is on the plain below it. Po Klong Garai, founded by the king who traded two provinces for a Vietnamese princess, is still a working temple. At Phan Rang two gravestones cut with Kufic Arabic and dated 1039 mark the tomb of a trader named Abu Kamil — which is why Cham communities here are Hindu Balamon and Muslim Bani to this day.

So the journey opens with the goods that arrived and closes at the grave of a man who stayed. The last evening is the Sacred Cham — a walk out to a private table, a Cham feast, and a blessing given by a Cham master under the stars. It is the only ceremony on the trip, deliberately, and it is the last thing that happens.
